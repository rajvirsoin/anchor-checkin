# The research behind Anchor

The evidence this app is built on: a year of building self-worth that doesn't depend on women, likes, or being the loudest man in the room.

Evidence strength is marked honestly:
**Strong** = meta-analyses or repeated RCTs. **Moderate** = good studies, some limits. **Weak** = early, small, or self-report. **Pop-psych** = a useful idea without real research behind it.

---

## 1. The core problem: self-worth that depends on outside approval

**Contingent self-worth** (Crocker & Wolfe 2001; Crocker & Park 2004). When a man's worth rides on a domain he can't control (approval, looks, being chosen), his self-esteem rises and crashes with every signal from that domain. Pursuing self-esteem has costs: more anxiety, more defensiveness, worse relationships, less learning. *Moderate to strong.*

**Fragile vs. secure self-esteem** (Kernis 2003). High self-esteem can still be fragile: it needs constant feeding and reacts to threat. Secure self-esteem is quiet and stable. The goal is secure, not high. *Moderate.*

**Self-compassion beats self-esteem** (Neff 2003, 2011; Neff & Vonk 2009). Self-compassion gives the benefits people want from self-esteem (resilience, motivation, less anxiety) without ranking yourself against others. Its three parts: mindfulness ("this is hard"), common humanity ("men go through this"), kindness ("what would I say to my brother?"). Meta-analysis of 27 RCTs (Ferrari et al. 2019): large drop in rumination (g = 1.37), moderate drops in depression and anxiety. *Strong.*

**Self-efficacy** (Bandura 1977). Confidence comes from evidence of your own ability, mainly "mastery experiences." Keeping promises to yourself is the most direct mastery experience there is. *Strong.*

**Compassionate goals vs. self-image goals** (Crocker & Canevello 2008). Going into an interaction trying to *be seen as* something raises anxiety and damages connection. Going in to *contribute or support* builds both. *Moderate.*

## 2. The pattern: perform or manage

**Anxious attachment** (Bowlby; Hazan & Shaver 1987; Mikulincer & Shaver 2007). A learned expectation that love is uncertain and must be earned or protected. Shows up as hypervigilance to signs of rejection, pleasing, swallowing needs, and seeking reassurance. **"Earned security"** is real: adults move toward secure attachment through reflection, therapy, and secure relationships (Roisman et al. 2002). *Moderate to strong.*

**Schema therapy** (Young, Klosko & Weishaar 2003). Early maladaptive schemas are deep beliefs formed in childhood. The ones this pattern usually involves are **abandonment**, **defectiveness/shame** ("not enough as I am"), and **approval-seeking**. Schema therapy has good RCT support for chronic patterns. Tools used in the app: schema flashcards and (with your therapist) imagery rescripting (Morina et al. 2017 meta-analysis, g = 0.90). *Strong in clinical settings.*

**Covert contracts** (Glover, *No More Mr Nice Guy*, 2000). "If I'm good, likeable, and helpful, I'll be loved and get my needs met without asking." Describes the perform-or-manage pattern closely. *Pop-psych*, but assertiveness training, which it overlaps with, has *moderate* support.

## 3. Feeling smaller around other men

**Social comparison** (Festinger 1954; Vogel et al. 2014). Upward comparison lowers self-esteem, and social media multiplies it. You compare your inside (doubt) with his outside (performance).

**Self-focus and safety behaviours** (Clark & Wells 1995; McManus, Sacadura & Clark 2008). In social anxiety, attention turns inward ("how am I coming across?") and people use safety behaviours (rehearsed jokes, scanning faces for approval, over-agreeing). In experiments, dropping them lowered anxiety **and** made people come across better. Cognitive therapy built on this is one of the best-evidenced treatments for social anxiety. *Strong.* This is the basis of the app's **drop-the-performance experiments**.

**Benign vs. malicious envy** (van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters 2009). Envy that points at self-improvement ("I want to grow that") motivates. Envy that wants to pull the other person down corrodes. You can steer toward the benign kind by naming the specific trait and the value behind it. *Moderate.*

**Golden shadow** (Jung; Johnson 1991). What you admire or envy in others is often a part of yourself you've disowned. *Pop-psych/theory*, used in the app as a reflection prompt, not as a fact.

**Male friendship.** Loneliness is a robust health risk (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010, 2015), and men are more likely to lack confiding friendships. Men's Sheds and men's groups show benefits for belonging and mood, but the evidence is weak and self-reported (Kelly et al. 2019). Close male friends turn other men from rivals into allies. *Moderate overall.*

## 4. The breakup

**Withdrawal** (Fisher et al. 2010). fMRI of people recently rejected in love showed activation in reward and craving regions, the same systems involved in addiction. That's why the "get her back" content pulls. *Moderate.*

**Self-concept clarity** (Slotter, Gardner & Finkel 2010). Losing your sense of "who I am" uniquely predicts breakup distress. Structured reflection, four times over nine weeks, sped recovery, and better self-concept clarity explained the gain (Larson & Sbarra 2015). *Moderate.* Basis of the **Who am I** check-in.

**Self-expansion after a breakup** (Lewandowski & Bizzoco 2007). After leaving a low-quality relationship, people rediscover and grow the self, usually noticeable after about three months. *Moderate.* Basis of **monthly quests**.

**Reappraising the ex** (Langeslag & Sanchez 2018). Thinking about the real downsides of the relationship lowered love feelings but made people feel worse. Distraction lifted mood. Use both, in short doses. *Moderate, lab study.*

**Self-compassion after separation** (Sbarra, Smith & Mehl 2012). Self-compassion predicted less distress up to nine months later. *Moderate.*

**Monitoring the ex online** (Marshall 2012). Checking an ex's profile is linked with worse recovery and more longing. *Moderate.*

## 5. Tools used in the moment (Catch)

**Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). "If X happens, then I do Y." 94 studies, d = 0.65. Turns willpower into automatic responses. *Strong.*

**Distanced (third-person) self-talk** (Kross et al. 2014). Talking to yourself by name ("Rajvir is feeling...") reduced anxiety and improved performance under social stress. *Moderate to strong.*

**Cyclic sighing** (Balban et al. 2023). Double inhale through the nose, long exhale through the mouth. Five minutes a day improved mood more than mindfulness meditation. *Moderate.*

**Urge surfing** (Marlatt; Bowen et al., mindfulness-based relapse prevention). Urges rise, crest, and fall without being acted on. Watching the curve teaches you that. *Moderate.*

**Affect labelling** (Lieberman et al. 2007). Naming the emotion lowers amygdala activity. Already part of Anchor's check-in. *Moderate to strong.*

**Feeling vs. story.** The therapy body skill: the feeling is physical and crests in minutes. The story is words with no floor. Consistent with ACT's "cognitive defusion" (Hayes et al.). *Moderate.*

## 6. Reflection practices (evenings)

**Expressive writing** (Pennebaker & Beall 1986; Frattaroli 2006). Writing about what hurts for 15 to 20 minutes on a few days. Effects are real but small on average. *Moderate, small.*

**Values affirmation** (Cohen & Sherman 2014). Writing about *why* a value matters to you before a threat buffers it. Works best timed before a known stressor. *Moderate.*

**Best possible self** (King 2001; Carrillo et al. 2019). Writing about your life when things have gone well raises positive mood and optimism (d about 0.3 to 0.5). Basis of the **year-end letter**. *Moderate.*

**Internal Family Systems** (Schwartz). Talking *to* a part ("the part of me that panics when she travels") instead of *being* it. Early RCT evidence. *Weak to moderate.*

**Gratitude and savouring** (Lyubomirsky et al. 2005; Dickens 2017). Small effects; weekly works better than daily, which wears off. *Weak to moderate.*

## 7. Biology

**Sleep** (Ben Simon & Walker 2018; Leproult & Van Cauter 2011). One night of sleep loss causes social withdrawal and makes others see you as lonelier. A week of 5-hour nights measurably lowers testosterone in young men. *Moderate to strong.*

**Resistance training** (Gordon et al. 2017, 2018). Meta-analyses: reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms. Frame lifting as keeping promises, not as becoming more attractive. *Strong.*

**Touch** (Cohen et al. 2015; Field 2010). Hugs buffered stress effects on getting sick. Massage lowers cortisol modestly. A year without romantic touch is a real gap to fill with safe touch. *Moderate.*

**Social media** (Hunt et al. 2018). Limiting to about 30 minutes a day for three weeks lowered loneliness and depression. Self-monitoring alone reduced anxiety and FOMO. *Moderate (small student sample).*

**Porn.** The sense of being "addicted" is driven more by moral incongruence and shame than by how much someone watches (Grubbs et al. 2019). A 7-day abstinence RCT found no average withdrawal effects (Fernandez et al. 2023). "Dopamine reset" claims are hype. Treated in the app as a values choice with compassion for slips. *Moderate.*

**Cold exposure.** Raises dopamine and noradrenaline (Šrámek et al. 2000), but a 2025 review of 11 studies found little evidence it improves mood (Cain et al. 2025). Optional, never a measure of manhood. *Weak for mood.*

**Power posing.** The hormone effects did not replicate (Ranehill et al. 2015). Left out.

## 8. What backfires (and how the app avoids it)

1. **"I am worthy" affirmations.** People with low self-esteem felt worse after repeating them (Wood, Perunovic & Lee 2009). *The morning promise is an action, never an identity claim.*
2. **A score you need approval from.** A metric of worth becomes a new contingency (Crocker & Park 2004). *The score counts actions, shows trends not daily grades, and says it measures practice, not your value.*
3. **Shame streaks.** NoFap-style counters feed the shame loop. *There's no streak. The app counts days you came back.*
4. **An AI that praises you.** It would replace women with a chatbot as the source of approval. *The coach reflects and asks. It never praises, flatters, or reassures.*
5. **Unlimited venting.** Rumination prolongs distress. *Coach chats are capped at 10 exchanges and end with one action.*

## 9. Why most apps get abandoned

Median 15-day retention for mental health apps is about 4% (Baumel et al. 2019). Most drop-off happens in the first ten days. What helps: no guilt for missed days (Finch's model, industry data), showing people their own words back to them, and fresh content over time. *The app welcomes you back after missed days, writes a monthly Then vs Now from your own quotes, and changes content each season.*
